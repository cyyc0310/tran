# V401: Shanghai local-generation data do not identify delivered-electricity CIF

Date: 2026-10-31. This is an information-boundary analysis, not an hourly prediction experiment.

## Observed public balance

Shanghai's official 2025 power-supply sustainability report states total electricity use of 2,089 (100 million kWh), local generation of 1,091 (52.2%), and imports from outside Shanghai of 998.4 (47.8%). It separately reports local renewable generation of 129.7 (100 million kWh; 11.9% of local generation) and renewable electricity consumed of 713 (34.1% of total use). The report is available from the [Shanghai Municipal Commission of Economy and Informatization](https://sheitc.sh.gov.cn/cmsres/ce/ce33b7d74e0046dca982386d323f0e39/fb01b230441d5192a60787746288f940.pdf). The 2024 report shows the same boundary distinction, with 45.3% of electricity use supplied from outside Shanghai.

The user's NBS exports describe local generation by technology. Shanghai hydro and nuclear generation are zero as reported; this does not mean imported electricity contains no hydro or nuclear. Therefore these data can support a local-generation intensity task but do not, by themselves, define the carbon intensity of electricity delivered to Shanghai consumers.

There is also a statistical-scope mismatch inside the local-generation boundary. Each supplied NBS export explicitly says the monthly energy-product series covers industrial legal entities above the designated revenue threshold. Its December 2025 cumulative values are 958.3 thermal, 26.2 wind, and 16.2 solar, all in 100 million kWh; the wind-plus-solar subtotal is 42.4. The full-scope Shanghai 2025 report gives local generation 1,091 and local renewable generation 129.7 in the same units. Thus the NBS wind/solar subtotal is 87.3 lower, or about 67.3% below the full-scope renewable figure; the NBS sum of the three reported technologies is 1,000.7, 90.3 below full-scope local generation. As a cross-check, full-scope nonrenewable generation implied by the report is 1,091−129.7=961.3, only 3.0 above the NBS thermal cumulative value. This arithmetic suggests that most of the aggregate scope gap lies in omitted renewable generation, consistent with the NBS scope note, but does not prove the exact technology or month allocation of the omitted amount.

This has a direct modeling consequence: treating the NBS five-category rows as a complete Shanghai production mix would overstate the thermal share and therefore bias an operational generation-CIF proxy upward. The public full-scope annual report supplies an annual correction anchor, but no monthly allocation of the missing 87.3; annual reconciliation can constrain the yearly total while leaving monthly/hourly intensity underidentified.

For scale, under the deliberately simplified factor box used in the project's identification work (thermal EF 400–1,000 g/kWh; reported renewables at zero operational EF; hydro/nuclear zero as reported), the NBS subtotal implies a thermal share of 95.76%, while the full-scope annual report implies 88.11% nonrenewable share. The corresponding NBS-only annual generation-CIF proxy exceeds the reconciled full-scope proxy by 30.6–76.5 g/kWh across that EF box (53.6 g/kWh at 700). These are conditional proxy calculations from rounded annual totals, not measured Shanghai CIFs. They show that even the production-side task has a material scope bias before adding the separate 47.8% imported-power uncertainty for the consumption-side task.

The annual residual also fails to determine a monthly correction. Treat the 87.3 renewable-generation gap as a nonnegative residual allocated over 2025 monthly buckets, constrained only by that annual sum and the official year-end renewable capacity of 8.42 GW. Use year-end capacity as a conservative upper bound for every month, and keep Jan–Feb together because the NBS current values are not separately reported. A residual of 87.3 can be assigned to any one ordinary month up to its 60.6–62.6 (100 million kWh) physical energy cap, while the remaining months have enough aggregate capacity to absorb the balance. At a fixed 700 g/kWh thermal factor, each Mar–Dec month therefore retains a feasible intensity spread of 233.9–318.8 g/kWh from this omitted-renewable allocation alone; the Jan–Feb combined bucket spread is 222.0 g/kWh. These are componentwise partial-identification widths under a loose capacity-only allocation, not probability intervals. They show that a public annual correction can fix the yearly mix while leaving monthly CIF largely unresolved without monthly full-scope renewable measurements or stronger temporal constraints.

## Identifiability calculation

At the reported annual balance, delivered average intensity would satisfy, approximately,

`I_delivered = 0.522 * I_local + 0.478 * I_imported`,

assuming the report's annual generation/import/use accounting aligns and transmission losses, storage, and accounting adjustments are negligible at this precision. This assumption is explicit; the figures are rounded.

Even granting an oracle for `I_local`, local generation statistics contain no information about `I_imported`. Under a broad operational factor support of 0–1,000 gCO2e/kWh, two systems with identical Shanghai local generation and total import volume but import intensities 300 and 800 differ in delivered intensity by `0.478 * 500 = 239 gCO2e/kWh`. If no source-specific import factor is known, the imported component alone permits a delivered-intensity width of as much as 478 gCO2e/kWh. This is an algebraic partial-identification result, not an empirical interval or a claim that either endpoint is a likely Shanghai value.

The user's monthly thermal/wind/solar CSVs therefore cannot identify a consumption-based monthly or hourly CIF without at least one further local information layer: (a) the time-aligned origin and source composition of imports, or (b) an independently observed delivered-power carbon factor / emissions series. Annual import share constrains a yearly average only; it cannot recover hourly import dispatch, losses, or which imported generation served a given hour.

## Literature and novelty boundary

The production-versus-consumption distinction and embodied carbon in interprovincial trade are established research topics. Recent China studies use electricity-extended input-output accounting for consumption factors and show that transmission changes provincial factors; another 2026 study models long-run grid factors under transmission scenarios. See [Energy Policy (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.enpol.2025.114907), [Journal of Environmental Management (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jenvman.2025.124644), and [Resources, Conservation & Recycling (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.resconrec.2025.108773). These studies make it inappropriate to claim that cross-boundary carbon flows themselves are a novel discovery.

The candidate contribution is narrower and empirically testable: a **release-time-, coverage-, and boundary-aware CIF transfer benchmark** that labels each prediction target as above-scale local generation, full-scope local generation, or delivered consumption; measures which public inputs are available at prediction time; reconciles annual anchors without silently fabricating monthly values; and quantifies how omitted generation and import information limit the sharpness of any estimate. The algorithmic question becomes whether a transferable model can produce valid, efficient intervals when the target boundary and reporting scope are explicit but part of the energy source mix is not. That must be tested against boundary-matched baselines and independent target observations; this note alone is not an algorithmic breakthrough or a paper-ready performance result.

## Next gate

Before another model search, define the target as either (1) production-side regional generation intensity, where the NBS composition is relevant but within-thermal fuel mix and hourly dispatch remain unknown, or (2) consumption-side/delivered intensity, where imported generation flow and source factors must enter the information contract. Run source-only feature-budget ablations separately for these targets. Do not score local-generation predictions against a delivered-consumption target or claim import-aware transfer without time-aligned import data.
